SECTION 1: DATA COLLECTION

1. The Process of Statistics

  • Statistics is the science of collecting, organizing, summarizing, and analyzing information to draw conclusions or answer questions.
  • Data are facts used to draw conclusions, and a key aspect is that data vary.
  • The 4 Steps: 1. Identify the research objective. 2. Collect the data. 3. Describe the data (Descriptive Statistics). 4. Perform inference to extend results to a population (Inferential Statistics).
  • Population vs Sample: Population is the entire group; a sample is a subset.
  • Parameter vs Statistic: A parameter summarizes a population; a statistic summarizes a sample.

2. Variables & Levels of Measurement

  • Qualitative: Classifies individuals based on attributes (e.g., Gender).
  • Quantitative: Numerical measures where math operations are meaningful. Can be Discrete (finite/countable) or Continuous (infinite/measured).

Levels of Measurement:

  • Nominal: Names/labels only. No ranking.
  • Ordinal: Can be ranked, but differences mean nothing.
  • Interval: Differences have meaning, but zero does NOT mean absence (e.g., Temperature).
  • Ratio: Ratios have meaning, and zero means true absence.

3. Observational Studies vs. Experiments

  • Observational Study: Measures response variables without trying to influence outcomes. Can only claim association, not causation. Types include Cross-sectional (specific point in time), Case-control (retrospective), and Cohort (prospective/over time).
  • Designed Experiment: Researcher assigns groups, intentionally changes the explanatory variable, and records responses.
  • Confounding: When effects of two or more explanatory variables are not separated.
  • Lurking Variable: An unaccounted variable that affects the response.

4. Sampling & Bias

  • Simple Random Sample (SRS): Every sample of size n has an equally likely chance of occurring.
  • Stratified Sample: Population is divided into nonoverlapping groups (strata), and an SRS is drawn from each.
  • Systematic Sample: Selecting every kth individual.
  • Cluster Sample: Selecting all individuals within a randomly selected group.
  • Multistage Sampling: A combination of techniques (e.g., Nielsen ratings).
  • Census: A list of all individuals in a population. Web Scraping: Extracting data from the internet.

Errors & Bias:

  • Sampling Error: Error resulting from using a sample to estimate a population.
  • Nonsampling Error: Errors from sampling bias, nonresponse bias, response bias, or data entry error.

SECTION 2: ORGANIZING DATA

1. Qualitative vs Quantitative Organization

  • Qualitative: Use frequency distributions, relative frequencies, bar graphs (bars don't touch), Pareto charts (bars in decreasing order), or Pie charts (area is proportional to frequency).
  • Quantitative: If continuous or large discrete, create Classes using intervals.
  • Class Width: The difference between consecutive lower class limits.
  • Use Histograms (bars touch), Dot Plots, or Time-Series plots (data over time connected by lines).

2. Shapes of Distributions & Misleading Graphs

  • Uniform: Frequencies are evenly spread out.
  • Bell-shaped: Highest frequency in the middle, tailing off symmetrically.
  • Skewed Right: Tail to the right is longer.
  • Skewed Left: Tail to the left is longer.
  • Misleading Graphs: Inconsistent scales, misplaced origin, 3D effects, or severe clutter.

SECTION 3: OGIVES & POLYGONS

1. Cumulative Distributions

  • Relative Frequency: Comparing relative instead of absolute frequencies allows for comparing data from different sizes of samples.
  • Frequency Polygon: Uses midpoints on the horizontal axis connected by straight lines. Add empty classes at ends to anchor to the axis.
  • Ogives: A graph of a cumulative frequency distribution.
  • For a "less-than" ogive, plot points showing the number of items with less value than the axis marker; the line slopes up and to the right.
  • Estimating the middle: Draw a horizontal line from 0.50 on a relative frequency ogive to the curve, drop down to the X-axis, and you can approximate the middle value of the original data.
